Seven ways to see two players

Candidates for the compare page, drawn on Jefferson, Addison and Jennings. Each one answers a different question. Pick the ones that earn a place; the rest stay in the drawer.

1Percentile radar

The eight league grades as a shape. Big and round is a complete player; a spike is a specialist. One polygon per player, so overlap and gaps read instantly.

Answers: what kind of player is each one, at a glance.

2Targets on one field

Both players' targets on the same field in slot colors, with the lane and depth split beside it. Where the dots mix they share a role; where only one color lives, that is his territory.

Answers: do they do the same job, or split the field.

3Depth butterfly

Targets by depth band, back to back. The solid part of each bar is the caught share. The widest band is where the offense goes to him; the gap between solid and faded is where it misses.

Answers: who gets the deep looks, and who actually catches them.

4The race

Cumulative yards week by week, one line per player. Byes and missed games show as flat steps. The gap between lines is the season in one number.

Answers: who is pulling away, and when it happened.

5Week by week, grouped

Yards per week as grouped bars, so the same Sunday sits side by side. Easier to read than the aligned table when the question is simply who had the bigger day.

Answers: who won each week.

6Where he wins

A three by three of the field, lanes across and depth down, each cell tinted by EPA per target with the target count underneath. One grid per player, same scale, so the hot cells compare straight across.

Answers: which spot on the field each one turns into points.

7Floor and ceiling

One box per week, colored by the day: green is a boom (top quarter of his position's weekly yards), amber is solid, red is a dud, grey is a bye or a missed game. The number is yards. Read across for consistency, down for who you would rather start.

Answers: who you can count on, and who swings.
